From the desk of Rob Brough, Sunshine Coast, Queensland

To the person who built this business,

You probably didn't land on this page looking for a film.

You landed here because somewhere in the back of your mind there's a thought most of the owners I talk to carry around, whether they say it out loud or not: the best thing about your business is the thing nobody gets to see.

Your customers see a logo, a phone number and a quote. Maybe a website that says "quality service, experienced team, family owned". The same few phrases sitting on your competitors' websites, almost word for word.

What they don't see is everything that actually makes you worth choosing. The year it nearly went under. The staff member who has been with you longer than some of your customers have been in business. The job you finished at your own cost because you'd given your word.

That's the part that would win them over. And it's the part that never makes it onto a brochure.

My name is Rob Brough. You may know my face, or at least my voice.

For more than 40 years my job was telling Queenslanders what happened that day. I started as a radio announcer in Charleville in the 1970s, moved into television sport in Brisbane in the 1980s, hosted Family Feud on Channel 7 in the early '90s, and then spent almost 20 years presenting the evening news for 7NEWS across regional Queensland, from the studio here on the Sunshine Coast.

I don't tell you that to impress you. I tell you because of what that job teaches you.

When you read the news every night, you learn quickly that you get a few seconds to make people care. Facts don't do it. People do.

A face. A voice. A moment where someone tells you what really happened, in their own words. That's what holds a viewer. It turns out it's exactly what holds a customer, too.

The night a story saved a charity swim

A few years ago a swimmer named Brendan came to me with a problem. The Island Charity Swim, a 10 kilometre swim around Mudjimba Island that had raised money for special schools on the Coast since 2001, was close to folding. He wanted its story told before it disappeared.

So I made a film. It's called Dare to Care. It runs 50 minutes, and it premiered at Kawana Event Cinemas.

The theatre sold out. The night raised $86,000.

Here's the part I want you to notice. Nothing about the swim changed between the week before that screening and the week after. Same swimmers, same cause, same island. What changed was that people finally saw why it mattered.

I watched the same thing happen with a group of Coolum surf lifesavers in a film called Old Mates. I was so moved by the way those men had looked after each other for a lifetime that I had to tell it. Today that film is shared on the website of Tremco Pipeline Equipment, a business that wanted its name alongside a story like that.

That's when it clicked for me. Every good business on this coast has a story like that. Almost none of them have ever told it.

Why a true story does what an ad can't

Let me be straight with you, because you've probably been sold to plenty already. Video on its own isn't magic. There is a mountain of business video out there that nobody watches past the first few seconds, because it's an ad pretending not to be one.

A true story is different, for three reasons.

It gives you credibility you can't buy. When you say you're good, it's marketing. When your customer says it, unprompted, looking down the lens, it's proof. In the films I make, your customer or your business is the hero. You're never the one doing the bragging, and that's exactly why people believe it.

It builds trust before the first phone call. People buy from people they feel they know. After a few minutes with your story, the people, the yard, the hard years, a stranger feels like they've met you. By the time they ring, half the selling is already done.

It sells, long after the shoot. Your film doesn't sit on a shelf. Your sales team sends it before a first meeting. It lives on your website and YouTube. And every film is cut into 12 short clips, so you have something real to post every month for a year.

You don't have to take my word for it. In a recent survey of marketers, 83% said video had directly increased their sales.

How I make sure the story actually sells

Here's the worry I hear most from owners: "What if we end up with a lovely film that doesn't do anything for the business?"

It's a fair question. That's why every film I make follows the same five beats. It's a method from Donald Miller's book Building a StoryBrand, and a lot of marketing teams already use it:

  1. The hero, and what they wanted.
  2. The problem that made it hard.
  3. The guide who backed them.
  4. How they did it.
  5. Where they are now.

It's the shape of almost every story you've ever loved. And it keeps the film about the business. The family history, the community, the footy club, all of it strengthens the story instead of taking it over.

Before I film a single frame, you get a one-page outline of those five beats: who says what, and why. You approve it. If something isn't right, we fix it on paper, not in the edit. No surprises for you, and no surprises for anyone else who has to sign off.

What it's actually like to work with me

You don't need to be good on camera. Most of the owners I meet would rather be on the tools than talking about themselves, and that's fine. Getting the story out of people is the thing I've done every working day for four decades. Mostly it's a cuppa and a yarn, and I keep the moments where you sound like yourself.

We film on weekdays, planned around your business, so nobody stops trading.

And I'll be honest about one more thing. I'm not a production house with a waiting room and a fleet of vans. I take on a handful of films a year, and I'm there for every one of them: the interviews, the filming and the edit. If that sounds like a limitation, it's also the reason the films are any good.

What happens if you leave it

Nothing dramatic. That's the problem.

You stay one more name on a quote list. Buyers keep comparing you on price, because they've been given nothing else to compare you on. And one day the people who know how this business was really built, the founder and the old hands, won't be around to tell it. That story retires when they do.

The good news is that it's easy to start, and the first step costs you nothing but five minutes.
