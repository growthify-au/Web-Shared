Feed Buying Guide

Why experienced producers never buy hay on looks alone - and what they check instead

Colour and smell tell you almost nothing about what a bale will do in a ration. Here's what a feed test reveals, why a Commodity Vendor Declaration matters more than a handshake, and the five things to demand before feed goes anywhere near your herd.

A guide from the team at FodderLink - Australia-wide fodder supply, Cabarlah QLD

The most expensive hay is the hay that lets your herd down

Every producer has heard a version of this story, and plenty have lived it. The photos looked great. The price was right. The seller seemed genuine. Then the load arrives and the cattle back off it, or worse - it's dusty, mouldy through the centre, or nothing like the bales in the picture.

By the time you know, the truck is gone, the money is paid, and there's no test data, no declaration and no comeback. There are documented cases of hay buyers losing thousands of dollars in payment scams on classifieds and social media - and even when the hay is real, what shows up is often not what was promised.

The uncomfortable truth is that visual appearance doesn't reliably reflect nutritional value. Hay that presents beautifully can under-deliver on protein and energy, and hay that looks ordinary can test remarkably well.

What a feed test tells you that your eyes can't

A NIR (near-infrared) feed test reports up to 30 nutritional values from a properly sampled batch - crude protein, metabolisable energy, fibre fractions, moisture and more. For anyone feeding cattle seriously, those numbers are the difference between formulating a ration and guessing at one.

This is why FodderLink doesn't list a batch without one. Every listing on the platform publishes its completed feed-test results, alongside something rarer: an honest, human inspection record.

When a marketplace publishes the unflattering notes too, you can trust the flattering ones. You're buying on facts, not photos.

The chemical history nobody asks about - until it's a problem

Here's the question too few buyers ask a private seller: what was sprayed on this, and when? Chemical treatments and withholding periods (WHP/ESI) matter - to your livestock's health and to your meat or milk market access.

FodderLink's rule is blunt: without a completed Commodity Vendor Declaration documenting chemical treatments and withholding data, a batch cannot be listed, marketed or purchased. Every batch, every time. It protects the animals, and it gives you a paper trail your processor will recognise.

The five things to demand before you buy any feed

Print this. Use it on anyone selling you hay.

  1. A completed feed test. Not "it tests well" - the actual NIR results for this batch: protein, energy, fibre, moisture.
  2. An inspection record with a name on it. Who looked at it, when, how many probes were sampled, and what they found - including the faults.
  3. A Commodity Vendor Declaration. The chemical treatment and withholding-period history, in writing.
  4. A published grade. Premium, Prime or Utility under a system you can read - so you can match quality to stock class and budget.
  5. A freight-inclusive quote. Hay is bulky; the landed cost is the only cost that matters. No surprises at the weighbridge.

Every FodderLink listing comes with all five as standard. Most private sales come with none of them.
